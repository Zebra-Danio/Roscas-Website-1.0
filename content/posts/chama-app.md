Ask anyone from Kenya and they'll tell you: the chama is not a financial product, it's a way of life. Hundreds of thousands of these groups run across Kenya and East Africa — friends, colleagues, church members and neighbours pooling money towards things no one could reach alone. Some are pure merry-go-rounds, where members contribute a fixed amount on a fixed rhythm and take turns to receive the whole pot. Some have grown into table-banking groups and investment clubs buying land and shares. And when Kenyans moved abroad, the chama moved with them — it is alive and well in London, Luton, Manchester and every town with a Kenyan church congregation or an NHS ward with Kenyan nurses on the rota.

Wherever it runs, one thing is constant: somebody keeps the books. Usually the treasurer. Often for years. Almost always unpaid. If that's you, this one is for you.

## The treasurer's evening

Chamas are famously well organised — a chairperson to keep the meeting in order, a secretary for the minutes, a treasurer for the money. It's one of the tradition's great strengths. But structure on paper doesn't shrink the work; it just gives it a job title.

The treasurer's real week looks like this. Updating the ledger after every contribution. Cross-checking the M-money confirmations and bank transfers against the list. Remembering that Wanjiru paid for two rounds before she travelled, and that Otieno swapped his turn with Achieng's because of school fees in January. Sending the reminders — worded gently, because these are your friends, your church, your cousins. And answering, again, the question every treasurer knows by heart: *"Umepokea yangu?"* — has mine arrived?

None of that is the chama. The chama is the trust, the meeting, the turn-taking, the harambee spirit of pulling together. The rest is bookkeeping — and it usually lives in an exercise book, a spreadsheet, or a WhatsApp thread eight hundred messages deep.

## Five habits of a chama that keeps its people

Groups that run for decades — and plenty do — tend to share the same habits, whether their records live on paper or on a phone.

**1. Write the rules down, even for a small group.** The big investment chamas have constitutions; a merry-go-round of eight friends needs only a page. Contribution amount, the rhythm, what happens when someone misses a round or leaves mid-cycle. Agreeing it before the first contribution is a conversation; agreeing it after a problem is a dispute.

**2. Share the offices — and rotate them.** A chairperson, a secretary and a treasurer exist so no one person carries the group. The chamas that burn out their best people are the ones where the same treasurer serves for ten years because nobody else can face the ledger.

**3. Fix the turn order where everyone can see it.** Draw numbers at the first meeting, go by seniority, or agree by need — the member with a wedding or a school-fees deadline takes an earlier turn. All of these work. What breaks a chama is an order that seems to shift in private.

**4. Collect on payday, not before it.** A contribution due just after month-end arrives on its own. One due just before month-end has to be chased. For members on NHS or care-sector rotas paid on the last working day, this single habit removes half the treasurer's reminders.

**5. Keep one record every member can check.** Most chama disputes are not about honesty — they're about two people remembering the same payment differently. Fines and penalties only feel fair when the ledger they're based on is one everybody trusts and everybody can see.

Do these five and your chama will outlast most — no app required.

## The ledger is the weak point

Look back at that list: four of the five habits rest on the record. And the record rests on one person. The exercise book lives in the treasurer's bag. The spreadsheet lives on their laptop. Members can't see either, so they message the treasurer — who becomes the single point of failure in a group whose entire purpose is shared strength.

The tradition isn't the problem. The tracking is.
