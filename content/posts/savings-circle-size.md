It is the first thing a new circle argues about and the last thing anyone writes down. Six people? Twelve? Everyone who asked to join?

Whatever tradition you are running — a **paluwagan**, an **ajo** or **esusu**, a **chama**, a **dhukuti**, a **pardner hand** — the number of members is not a headcount. It is the single decision that fixes almost everything else about how the circle will feel to be in.

## The arithmetic nobody writes down

Two lines, and they settle the rest:

**Members × contribution = the pot.**
**Members × interval = the length of the cycle.**

Ten people at £100 a month is a £1,000 pot and a ten-month cycle. Twenty people at £100 a month is a £2,000 pot — and a cycle that runs into a second year, with someone waiting twenty months for their turn.

You cannot grow the pot without growing something else: either the contribution goes up, or the wait does. Every argument about circle size is really an argument about which of those three the group is willing to move — and it goes better when everyone can see that plainly rather than sensing it.

## What the number is really choosing

**The pot.** More members, more money at each turn. This is why people want big circles, and it is a good reason.

**The wait.** An early turn is effectively an interest-free advance; a late turn is pure saving with a lump sum at the end. Both are fine — and both get more extreme as the circle grows.

**The exposure.** The last member depends on everyone ahead of them still being there when their turn comes. In a ten-month circle that is nine people for most of a year; in a twenty-four-month circle it is twenty-three people for two years, through job changes and house moves. Longer circles are not riskier because people are less honest. They are riskier because life is longer.

**The admin.** The record grows in step with the members. The chasing grows faster, because it is driven by how many people can be late at once.

## The sizes circles actually land on

**Small — roughly four to eight.** Finishes fast, often inside half a year, and everyone genuinely knows everyone. The pot is modest, which is right when the goal is modest: a course deposit, a flight home, a new washing machine. The best shape for a group's first circle together.

**Middle — roughly nine to fifteen.** The shape most traditions settle into: a monthly circle completes inside a year and the pot is large enough to matter. It is also the point at which the organiser stops being able to hold it all in their head.

**Large — sixteen and up.** These work, and always have — but look at where they work. Behind almost every long-running large circle is something already holding the group together: a church, a workplace, an association, an extended family. That institution does part of the job familiarity does in a small circle. If your group has it, go ahead. If it does not, the size is borrowing trust the group has not built yet.

## Four questions before you fix the number

**1. What is the pot actually for?** Work backwards from the goal, not forwards from the guest list. If members want £800 each, you do not need twenty people — you need to solve for £800 and stop.

**2. How long will everyone realistically stay together?** Workplace circles are the ones to watch: a group that feels permanent in March can lose three members to new jobs by Christmas, and they will still owe their contributions.

**3. Would member twelve vouch for member three?** In most circles trust runs through the organiser rather than between every pair of members — people join because they trust you. That works, but it means a bigger circle asks more of your judgement, not less.

**4. Can you actually run it at that size?** The honest answer sets a ceiling, and there is no shame in it. Two circles of eight, run well, beat one of sixteen run at the edge of what one person can carry.

## Four ways to get a bigger pot without a bigger circle

**Shorten the interval.** Ten members contributing weekly is a ten-week cycle rather than a ten-month one — same people, same pot, a quarter of the calendar wait. Weekly contributions suit weekly wages, which is why so many circles run that way.

**Raise the contribution instead of the headcount.** Eight at £150 beats twelve at £100 on both pot and cycle length. One caution worth more than the rest of this article: set the amount at what the least comfortable member can manage every round, not at what the most comfortable member suggests.

**Run two circles rather than one big one.** Two groups of eight, side by side or one after the other, serve sixteen people while keeping the trust dense and the admin sane.

**Let a trusted member take two slots.** Common in many traditions, and it works — but be honest about what it is. That member contributes double every round and owes two turns of obligation, so it suits someone with a completed cycle behind them.

## When in doubt: smaller and shorter

A first circle has a job beyond the money: to finish. A completed cycle makes the next one easy to fill — people paid out on time recruit for you without being asked. You can always run it again, bigger.
