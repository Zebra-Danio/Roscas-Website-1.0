Before most British banks would say yes to a Caribbean customer, the pardner already had.

Call it a **pardner**, a **partner**, or a **padna** — in Trinidad and much of the Eastern Caribbean it's a **sou-sou**, in Ghana a **susu**, in Guyana a **box hand**. The shape is the same everywhere: a group of people who trust each other, a fixed amount each week or month — the **hand** you throw — and the whole pot — the **draw** — going to one member at a time until everyone has had their turn. No interest, no paperwork, no permission needed from anyone outside the circle.

And at the centre of it, one person: the **banker**. Usually unpaid, often a woman, frequently the person the whole street knows can be trusted. This article is for her — and for anyone carrying that book today.

## The hand that helped build Black Britain

The pardner deserves to be named for what it did. When the Windrush generation arrived in the 1950s and 60s, many high-street banks would not lend to them — some would barely serve them. So communities in Brixton, Handsworth, Moss Side, Chapeltown and St Pauls did what they had always done back home: they threw a hand.

Pardner money put deposits on houses when no mortgage was offered. It paid the passage for children and parents left behind. It bought sewing machines, stocked market stalls, covered funerals with dignity, and carried families through weeks when wages didn't stretch. Ask around any long-standing Caribbean community in Britain and you will find homes that exist because a banker kept a clean book and twenty people never missed a week.

That history is why the tradition is still running, generations on — in church congregations, among NHS colleagues, in family WhatsApp groups. The pardner never needed rescuing. It works.

## What a good banker actually carries

Anyone who has run a pardner hand knows the job is bigger than it looks from outside. In most circles it comes down to a few quiet responsibilities, carried every single week:

**The book.** Who has thrown their hand, who hasn't yet, whose draw came and went. In many pardners this is literally a notebook, guarded like a passport — because it is the only record there is.

**The draw order.** Who receives first, who receives last, and what was agreed at the start. In many circles newer members wait longer for their draw while they earn their standing, and in some the banker takes the first or the final draw themselves — customs vary, and the right version is whatever your circle agreed openly at the beginning.

**The questions.** "When is my draw?" "Did Marcia throw her hand this week?" "What did we say happens if someone's late?" Every one of those questions comes to the banker, because only the banker can see the book.

**The chase.** The gentle Sunday-evening reminder that keeps a late hand from becoming a missing one — done kindly, done privately, done again next week.

None of that is a flaw in the tradition. But notice something about the last two jobs: they exist only because the record lives in one place and one pair of hands. The questions and the chasing aren't the pardner — they're the admin around the pardner.

## Keeping the book clean, whatever you keep it in

Three habits that long-running pardners tend to share — useful whether your book is paper, a spreadsheet, or anything else:

**Write the whole draw order down at the start, where every member can see it.** Not just "you're after Yvonne" — the full list, first draw to last. Most disputes are not about the money; they're about what people remember differently.

**Confirm every hand the day it lands.** A one-line acknowledgement — received, recorded — closes the gap where doubt grows. Silence is where trust leaks.

**Agree the what-ifs before they happen.** What happens if a hand is late? Can two members swap draws? Who can bring in a new member, and who vouches for them? A pardner that answers these questions in week one almost never has to answer them in anger in week twenty.
